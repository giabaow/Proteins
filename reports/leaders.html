This company develops mass photometry instruments that measure the mass of single biomolecules in solution in their native state without labels. Their systems are used for characterizing proteins, nucleic acids, AAVs, antibodies, VLPs, and membrane proteins.

United Kingdom · multi-omic

Oxford Nanopore provides nanopore-based sequencing technology that enables biological analysis across genomics, epigenomics, and transcriptomics. Their platforms are used by researchers and clinicians worldwide to explore biological questions.

The leader to study

Gordon SangheraFounder and former CEO (held CEO post until 2026, now advisory role)

Co-founded Oxford Nanopore (as Oxford Nanolabs) in 2005 with Dr Spike Willcocks and Professor Hagan Bayley, with seed funding from IP Group plc. Brought experience combining biological and electronic technologies; previously delivered blood glucose-sensing products to market, a technology that transformed diabetes patient care worldwide. Guided the company from single-channel research chemistry through to a commercialised, disruptive sequencing platform, before handing over to Francis Van Parys.

Why Oxford Nanopore is the clearest precedent for a physics-based, single-molecule sensing platform (nanopore + electronic current read-out) that started from academic IP, built proprietary hardware/chemistry over years, and scaled from single-channel devices to a fully commercial, multi-scale platform (MinION to PromethION) serving research first and now moving into regulated clinical diagnostics (e.g. VCGS ISO 15189 accreditation). Its trajectory from founder-led deep-tech spinout to a multi-omic, real-time sequencing leader with global partnerships is directly analogous to the path Proteins.1 could follow.

What made them succeed
  • Founding on strong academic IP and multidisciplinary academic collaborationFounded in 2005 by Gordon Sanghera, Spike Willcocks and Hagan Bayley, combining Bayley's protein nanopore work with IP and expertise from Branton, Deamer and Akeson at Harvard, Boston University and UC Santa Cruz.
  • Early seed funding from a specialist IP-commercialisation investorSeed funding from IP Group plc enabled the company to spin out of academic research as Oxford Nanolabs.
  • Recruiting proven sequencing-commercialisation talentIn 2008, Dr John Milton and Clive G. Brown joined the executive team, bringing prior experience developing DNA sequencing technology at Solexa, which was acquired and commercialised by Illumina.
  • Staged hardware scaling before broad commercial launchThe company progressed from single-channel Axopatch devices to 4-, 9-, then 128-channel sensor chips and bespoke ASICs before building the large-scale arrays used in today's commercial products.
  • Public proof-of-concept at a flagship scientific conference ahead of commercial rolloutIn February 2012 at the AGBT conference, Oxford Nanopore presented the first-ever nanopore sequencing data and an overview of the GridION and MinION hardware/software, building field credibility before market entry.
  • Real clinical/regulatory partnership milestones after research tractionVCGS achieved ISO 15189 accreditation for whole genome sequencing using Oxford Nanopore technology, and Oxford Nanopore expanded its collaboration with MATRIX to implement rapid CNS tumour classification across Norway.

  • Publish first proof-of-concept data at a leading field conference (analogous to ONT's AGBT 2012 debut) to build scientific credibility with the protein/biomarker research community before broad launch.
  • Position the enzyme-free, physics-based sensing method as a 'richer insights, one-platform' story (multi-biomarker DNA/RNA/protein in a single run) the way ONT markets its 'multiomic view' replacing multiple assays.
  • Target a specific high-value use case in oncology or neurology (e.g., an early biomarker panel) for a pilot partnership with a clinical genomics/diagnostics lab, using ONT's VCGS ISO 15189 accreditation and MATRIX CNS-tumour collaboration as the template for moving research use into regulated diagnostics.

  • Secure a specialist deep-tech/IP-focused seed investor (akin to IP Group plc backing ONT) to fund the multi-year hardware/chemistry development runway before commercial revenue.
  • Pursue a flagship clinical genomics/diagnostics lab partnership for accreditation (ISO 15189-style) as a beachhead into regulated diagnostics, following the VCGS precedent.
  • Form a collaboration with a hospital network or reference lab for a specific rapid-turnaround clinical application (e.g., rapid biomarker panel for time-critical treatment decisions), mirroring the Oxford Nanopore-MATRIX CNS tumour classification expansion in Norway.

Proteins.1 should follow Oxford Nanopore's precedent: build on strong founding IP with staged hardware/chemistry scaling, first proving the enzyme-free platform in research-use applications (oncology, neurology, immunology) to accumulate peer-reviewed validation, much as ONT did before its clinical push. Recruit executives with prior single-molecule sequencing/diagnostics commercialisation experience, and pursue a landmark academic/clinical partnership (like VCGS's ISO 15189 accreditation or the MATRIX CNS-tumour collaboration) as the bridge from research tool to regulated diagnostic product.

Attomarker develops point-of-care antibody testing devices, including a Triple Antibody Test for COVID-19 that detects antibodies to three different viral proteins in seven minutes.

The leader to study

Andrew ShawCEO & Founder, Attomarker; Associate Professor of Physical Chemistry, University of Exeter

Leads the University of Exeter research laboratory (Prof Shaw's research laboratory) whose translational research over many years produced the gold-nanoparticle plasmon multiplex platform; personally led the team that ran the first patient trial at St Thomas' Hospital in March 2020 and is credited in company materials as the scientist behind the technology's development into the Triple Antibody Test.

Why Attomarker shows a clear, repeatable playbook for a university spin-out with a physics-based, label-free multiplex protein/antibody detection platform: fast pivot of an EPSRC-funded research idea into a real-world clinical validation (St Thomas' Hospital) within weeks of a crisis, rapid regulatory clearance (MHRA/CE/UKCA), a manufacturing partnership with an established industrial player (Smiths Detection) to scale production, and now diversification into a broader immune-profiling pipeline (Long COVID endotyping, liver, fertility, allergy) plus non-dilutive/crowd equity funding routes (Exeter alumni appeal, Crowdcube Acceleration Round) and disease-area co-development deals (EpitogenX). This is directly analogous to Proteins.1's path from research-use biomarker detection toward regulated diagnostics.

What made them succeed
  • Rapid real-world clinical validation partnership with a major hospitalAttomarker printed gold arrays with minimal validation and began a collaboration with St Thomas' Hospital on 23rd March 2020, obtaining first patient results the same afternoon.
  • Peer-reviewed publication to support credibility and regulatory submissionResults from the St Thomas' pilot (119 tests, 74 patients) were published in the Royal Society of Chemistry journal Analyst, later supplemented by a second paper with 200 more patients showing up to 96% sensitivity.
  • Regulatory clearance milestone (MHRA authorisation) before scale-upThe Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) approved Attomarker's Triple Antibody Test for NHS use in the UK following the St Thomas' trial.
  • Manufacturing partnership with an established industrial player to scale device productionSmiths Detection collaborated with Attomarker since April 2020, manufacturing 10 pre-production devices at its Hemel Hempstead site with ability to ramp up volume, each device testing up to 50 blood samples per day.
  • Non-dilutive community/alumni funding to bootstrap the first clinical trialThe trial was fully funded by the University of Exeter's COVID-19 Emergency Appeal which raised a record £120,000 from over 1,000 alumni.
  • Disease-area co-development deal to extend the platform beyond the original indicationAttomarker recently signed a strategic co-development agreement with EpitogenX to leverage their next generation antigen technology, targeting Lyme disease and Hashimoto's disease.
  • Equity crowdfunding as a growth-stage financing routeAttomarker launched an Acceleration Round hosted on Crowdcube, the UK's regulated equity crowdfunding platform, to support commercial rollout and regulatory progress.
For Proteins.1
Applications to consider
  • Run a rapid 'crisis-anchored' pilot at a single leading hospital or research centre with a small, well-characterised patient cohort to generate a first real-world dataset quickly, as Attomarker did at St Thomas' within days of first contact.
  • Publish early pilot data in a peer-reviewed analytical chemistry/diagnostics journal (e.g. Analyst-type venue) to build third-party credibility ahead of any regulatory submission.
  • Seek a co-manufacturing partner with existing precision-instrument production capability (akin to Smiths Detection) to move from lab prototype to a robust, scalable point-of-need device rather than building manufacturing in-house.
  • Design the platform around a flexible, swappable biomarker panel (as Attomarker did, adding CRP, PAG, and viral proteins rapidly) so the same core physics engine can be re-targeted across oncology, neurology and immunology research panels without a full redesign.
  • Pursue a low-cost, non-dilutive proof-of-concept funding source (alumni/community appeal, translational grant) to fund the first pilot study before seeking larger venture or crowdfunding rounds.
  • Consider equity crowdfunding (e.g. Crowdcube-style rounds) once initial clinical/regulatory milestones are hit, as a route to fund late seed/Series A commercial rollout while building public visibility.
Market-route moves
  • Start in research-use-only sales to academic and hospital labs performing biomarker discovery, using early pilot partnerships (hospital collaborations) as reference sites, mirroring Attomarker's St Thomas' Hospital entry point.
  • Target a first regulatory clearance in a single high-need, well-defined indication (e.g. a specific oncology or neurology biomarker panel) via a fast-track pathway analogous to Attomarker's MHRA/CE Triple Antibody Test authorisation, rather than attempting broad clinical diagnostics clearance at once.
  • License or co-develop with an established diagnostics/instrumentation manufacturer for device production and distribution once a validated assay exists, following the Smiths Detection manufacturing partnership model.
  • Expand indication-by-indication through targeted co-development deals with antigen/reagent specialists (as with EpitogenX) to reach underserved disease markets (e.g. specific neuro-immunology or oncology biomarkers) without needing to build every assay internally.
  • Once clinical validation and initial regulatory milestones are achieved, use a phased financing approach: philanthropic/alumni-style seed funding for pilot studies, followed by an equity crowdfunding or accelerator round to fund commercial rollout and further regulatory work.
Suggested sequence

Follow Attomarker's precedent: secure a fast, low-cost pilot at a marquee clinical site to generate real-world validation data, publish it to build scientific credibility, and use that evidence to obtain an initial regulatory clearance in a narrowly defined indication. Then partner with an established instrumentation manufacturer (as Attomarker did with Smiths Detection) to scale device production, while expanding the biomarker panel indication-by-indication through targeted co-development deals (as with EpitogenX) and financing growth through a mix of philanthropic seed funding and equity crowdfunding.

The leader to study

Ulrich RantCo-founder & CEO of Dynamic Biosensors from its founding in 2012 until the 2024 Bruker acquisition; now Chief Scientific Officer of Bruker Biosensors

Rant led Dynamic Biosensors from inception, turning what colleagues describe as 'seemingly crazy ideas to analyze molecular interactions' into commercial products. The company itself was a spinout from Technical University of Munich (TUM), based on research jointly conducted by TUM and Fujitsu, developing the switchSENSE electro-switchable DNA nanolever biosensor technology. After leading the company through product development, commercial expansion and the Bruker acquisition, Rant moved from CEO to CSO to refocus on science and technology.

Why Dynamic Biosensors is a strong role-model precedent for Proteins.1 because it is a university deep-tech spinout (TUM/Fujitsu, comparable to a VTT spinout) that built a physics-based, label-agnostic biosensor platform (DNA nanolever electro-switching rather than enzymatic amplification) reaching femtomolar sensitivity across proteins, DNA and RNA - conceptually parallel to Proteins.1's enzyme-free, physics-based single-molecule platform. It scaled purely through Research Use Only sales into academic and pharma R&D (oncology, immunology, neurology, drug discovery), built credibility via peer-reviewed publications with top academic labs, raised staged VC rounds from regional/deep-tech investors, and ultimately exited via strategic acquisition into a major analytical-instruments company (Bruker) to gain global commercial reach - a plausible playbook for Proteins.1 to follow from seed stage to scale.
